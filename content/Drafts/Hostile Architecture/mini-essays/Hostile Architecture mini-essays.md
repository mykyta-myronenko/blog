Each mini-essay focuses on a single idea, which would then contribute to the bigger written piece. It gives me room to drill down, focus and understand the core (atomic) concepts.

The mini-essay might end up as a footnote of the larger project, it could be linked to, or mentioned in passing. If someone wants to learn more about a point I am making it remains available for reading and expands on an idea I am making, but doesn't have to bog down the "pacing" of the main article. Mini-essays is what would end up in a dissertation appendix.

It is also good to have the mini-essays be valuable on their own without having to fully understand the whole wider topic, and that these provide some entertainment value to the reader.

In the end publishing mini-essays is a pretty good way to share short-form written content and get some feedback on it, while I work on a bigger piece. It's definitely possible to publish a small essay per day.

# Structure and Constraints

- Communicate a single, simple, relevant, evidenced and unique thought.
- Keep these short (100-500 words) and don't spend a lot of time (10-20 minutes of writing is a good rule of thumb).
- Give an example of applicable.
- Illustrate it with an image (this is for the blog).
- Include external references, and link to other clearly relevant mini-essays.

## Styles

### Classic three-act essay

Intro, body, and conclusion.

#### Intro

This is where you hook the reader. Whether the reader is someone else or yourself in the future, making a clear intro that immediately sparks curiosity about the subject is crucial.

#### Body

Here you get to expand, explain and walk the reader through the thought process.

#### Conclusion

To make sure the idea sticks with the reader it's important to stick the landing with a satisfying conclusion.

You could summarise, but avoid repetition because it's such a short piece so there is no need for a long recap.

It is perfectly fine to keep it to a short few sentences, and leave your reader with a message that resonates or makes them think. Reframing the message is one way to achieve this.

### Fictional story to illustrate the point

Even for non-fiction mini-essays you use the common story-telling techniques are powerful. Some ideas are expressed best when you put the reader into the action and let them feel the emotion and drama.

Telling a story is a great way to get a point across in a vivid and entertaining way.

### Hybrid

Mixing the styles into a hybrid format is fine too. You don't need to restrict yourself to making a new fictional story or to keep your writing dry and factual. We are aiming for clarity that sparks curiosity, and the style is only another tool.

You can use the story part to give the emotion, illustrate, carry the message, and later expand beyond the fiction with the informative section.

# Examples of good and bad mini-essays

A good mini-essay makes a succinct point, and is specific. The scope should be quite narrow without making sweeping claims. And the claim should be substantiated with examples, sources, and data to justify and illustrate the point to the reader.

> [!fail] Example of a topic that is too shallow:
> Blue lights in bathrooms is repeatedly proven to be ineffective in solving illegal drug use in public spaces.

On the first glance, this seems to work. It ticks the boxes above, however, there is no unique thought here. This is a case of regurgitating articles into a summary without arriving to a conclusion.

> [!success] Example of a well formed mini-essay topic:
> Public spaces that are designed to displace illegal drug users are not solving the issue at hand, but only relocates the issue elsewhere.

This is an iteration of the previous example which I think would work better. The scope is a bit broader - perhaps it's starting to push outside of a mini-essay scope, and could remain focused on blue lights only, or the title re-phrased a bit - but the new thesis begins to synthesis several points into a new thought.

The points that the essay is making:

- There is a type of designed space that displaces people, and/or spaces can be designed to deliberately displace people.
- Design that displaces only moves into a different space (sweeping under the rug).
- Design that displaces does not solve these issues. Although this is a point that would need further substantiating.

What might be undefined:

- What's a public space?
- Can there _not_ an instance where displacing is the preferred solution?

To illustrate I would include concrete examples of "hostile design" that displaces and fails at solving the issues.

Compared to the first version, I'd say it's a step up in terms of original thought.

> [!fail] Example of a topic that is clearly too broad:
> Hostile Architecture fails to achieve what it's set out to do.

The previous example is border-line too broad, and would benefit from breaking it up or linking out to smaller sub-essays or satellite mini-essays.

This one however is clearly too broad, because requires a lot of definition and justification to even begin to make a point.

- What is Hostile Architecture?
- What is it setting out to achieve?
- Why and How does it fail?
- How does a design fail or succeed?
