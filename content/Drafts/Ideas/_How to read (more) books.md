There are only so many books that you will read in your lifetime and the number is scarily low. If you think of all the books your local library or a bookshop might have (let's say 50,000) you realistically might get through 10%, given you aren't a full-time book reviewer.

I, as a relatively slow reader, can reasonably read 50 books a year, so during my remaining lifetime - 50 years hopefully - I'll read 2500 books. This is a depressingly low amount if you think about it. I could maybe fill two office walls with these.^[Of course, really, the number matters very little. It's a consumerist lens to view reading, or learning through. Maybe it's even greedy to try to read more. Or there is something that scares me on an existential level that I can quantify my life by visualising two middle-sized bookshelves. Still though, I believe that we are what we eat, or more broadly consume. %%there is too much out there to experience so we have to find a way to focus on things that matter, to make decisions and systemic (systems thinking) habits/approaches(?) that funnel ideas in a way what is valuable to us%% We are the average of our 5 closest friends, and we are made by the 'content' we consume, so I think it's important to regiment your diet (including media consumption).]

How then do you then maximise the number of books, and increase the potential value you might get from them? I operate with two rules in mind.

%%an exercise to jump between books (spaced repetition like way of reading) and stop when you get stuck. can be counterproductive in a way - avoiding challenge.%%

1. Many inputs and a strong filter.

%%you don't know what you don't know%%

Try out many books; buy them, go to libraries, borrow books from friends, get a taster in bookshops or book cafes, read excerpts online (legally of course), listen to audio book previews, read parts on kindle-like platforms - you get the idea.

But! Don't feel like you have to stick with the book. If the book is not clicking for you or you are not enjoying it, not funding any value, I see no reason to stick with it, complete it, power through.

I always feel an obligation to stick reading, partially this can be because I like to own (hence buy) physical books, partially out of FOMO (what if in chapter 37 out of 45 I find a life-changing revelation!) but partially out of respect for the author%%and there is some ego in there too, I don't want to feel like a quitter when I don't finish things%%. I think this respect is a fallacy. If a book is not doing it for you, move on, you can always come back at a more opportune time, and sometimes that time will be never, and that is just fine.

Okay, but how do you _decide_ when a book is worth it. Well what helps me is the second rule.

1. A usefulness-entertainment matrix.

%%this is where the strong filter part feeds in%%

|               |                  |
| ------------- | ---------------- |
| Useful + Fun  | Useful + Boring  |
| Useless + Fun | Useless + Boring |

A read might fall within one of the four quadrants. If it's fun and useful, it's likely an easy read with a lot of insight, it's probably a good idea to continue and finish the book.

If a book is difficult but you can tell that it is generating many ideas, leading you to interesting places, surprises you, maybe it's relevant for a project you are currently or may work on in the future, _then_ it could be worth reading it fully or _in part_, even if it's not the most bombastic experience. If it's dull but very useful, so long as you can get through it and get something out of it, there is likely value in sticking with it.

On the topic of reading _in part_, you don't have to read the whole book (at once) (or at all). Treating some books as you would textbooks could be valuable. For instance, when I get stuck or struggle to connect the ideas in a non-fiction book, I try to map it out first. I read the introduction, the conclusion, skim the ideas. Look through different section of the index, and try to get the general idea/context. If it's a non-fiction that has some 'pre-requsits^[i.e. The Unbearable Lightness of Being references Nietzsche [on the first page] so to fully understand you might want to know more about the philosopher]' it could be valuable to look into those, or highlight and skip for later. Reading in several passes is another strategy, first you map and skim, them you might focus on the content and after, with the benefit of wider context of the book, you dive into individual concept to link the ideas into a more holistic picture.

If the book is enjoyable but doesn't teach you a whole lot practical, it could be still a useful read. I adore fiction personally; it benefits my well-being, it's always interesting to see how a good writer weaves a story and keeps a reader engaged, and it's just fun to visit a foreign world or live through an experience as someone else.

I think the most interesting quarter is the 'boring and pointless' one. If it brings you no joy, and you are finding little benefit in sticking with it, move on. And when it comes to FOMO, at this point, when you are on a brink of dropping the read, there is no shame in reading a summary, some reviews, maybe a discussion or talking to someone in your friends circle who has read it. Perhaps a new perspective will open your mind in some way and help you appreciate it more. Or maybe it will confirm that you should stop and move on.

Some reads \*\*cough\* like... most self-help books \*cough\** are pretty much made to suck up your attention by taking a handful of salient ideas and *streetttchinggg\* them across a decent 300 page tome. Look up a service that shows you the percentage of filler or some reviews to get the idea. These books might have value but it's often limited to a portion (like introduction or first third maybe, at least from my experience).
