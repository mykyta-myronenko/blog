The idea of making my own films has been somewhat of an obsessive nagging itch for the past few months (as of [[2024-05-17]]).

Ever since I saw what Joel Have has been doing with so little it seems so attainable to make films by yourself, yet I'm terrified of giving it a shot. So I thought, why not break it down into simpler components.

> [!note] A note to self:
> Don't forget that you need not master or even succeed in all the parts of the process to create something of artistic value. You need almost nothing to make anything. But if it helps you be more at ease, feel free to break it up.

# Film Stages

1. Explore your thoughts, play with ideas, occupy places, try art styles, act out characters. Think about your past memories but also live new ones, walk in the world, travel to a new borough, city or country. Open yourself up for novel experiences.
2. Pick an idea and develop it into a concept. Try asking 'what if **_ (but _** )?'
3. Break it into a story; outline it; storyboard. Having a map or a list of events is helpful to structure and organise your thoughts on the page.
4. Optionally, clean up the story, refine the script (places, action, dialogue), plan the little details for ease of mind but have a cut off point. Don't lose the idea to procrastination. Don't delay creating it.
5. Prepare. Collect all that is needed. Rehearse. Get the costumes and props, pick out locations and plan travel, get the reference footage, arrange with other people, find the right time of day / free time in the week. This is just the logistics. It's not complicated if you break it down and think through. In the end having all that you need for the production will relieve the anxiety on the day. By then you have it all figure out, no need to worry about wasting time, or failing to achieve something. You've prepared all you could think of.
6. Record. Set up the camera, print out the script or memorise your lines, strap on the mic and record. Pick a safe area to do this until you are more confident. Just start.
   - The planning is done, you know the scenes, you have the words, if you storyboard you even know the camera angles. You've lived with your characters and in your worlds, now share it with the world.
7. Perform. Until you've others to work with you get to be the lead, have all the control, really explore the characters and yourself. This can be performing in real life or voice acting for animation. Get comfortable with the character. It's best if the location feels safe, where you won't be interrupted or judged, at least for the first several projects. And remember you don't have to get it right on the first time. Try, and try. Maybe pick a different spot. Simplify dialogue, feel free to improvise. Rehearse if you must. Simplify it for yourself.
   - It is an ongoing process and it is through doing that you gain confidence and expertise. Consider [[How to get better at improv]] or taking acting classes to structure your learning, and get better at it everyday.
8. Edit. This is where you have the opportunity to bring your vision to life. You have all the pieces on the chopping board, and now you get to spend as much time as you want to / can allot.
9. Iterate. Bugfix. Solve the problems. You can apply this at each step of the way to improve what you have already. Keep at it. Try, try, and try again.

These are the steps that I came up with on the spot, but they are just one way of breaking up the process. They don't even reflect the reality of industry production and it doesn't matter. Just getting to a place of making stuff is what's important.

# Mixed Pipeline

Something to note is that, since you are producing this by yourself you don't have to follow a strict order. You can tackle multiple stages at once, and get the benefit out of this synergy.

You might get an idea for a character and could start acting immediately. That might help write the dialogue, and inform the story. You could build it up from there by figuring out the script, and prepare for the shoot.

It could be quite helpful, for example, to have your storyboard feed into the final edit which you fill out as you go along. This way you can assemble the footage and get the rough cut as you are shooting. And you get to see the film come to life, and your progress materialise on daily basis.

Because you have direct communication between the writers, direction, and editors this messy process might open up unique opportunities to improvise with direction on the day, fix mistakes, do reshoots, get more footage, etc. It also gives you a more varied workflow, which I think is fun.

Of course, there are parts that require more focus and are probably best done without jumping around. Particularly the writing and the final-edit stages. These two bookend the production process and where you have the most control, but really what's sandwiched in the middle can be mixed up into a blended workflow.

# Don't overthink it

You are in a position where you nobody expects quality out of you. Stop the self-judgement and worrying about whether it will turn out well, or if you are wasting time. The biggest waste of time is being scared to try. Might as well just go for it.
